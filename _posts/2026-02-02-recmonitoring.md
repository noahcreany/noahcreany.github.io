In my recent paper ([Creany et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jort.2025.101008)), I discussed the increasing abstraction in approaches to visitor use monitoring that have drifted further away from monitoring actual recreation use and instead focus on proxies for recreation use like tweets, publicly shared photos, and mobile device data. While these _novel data_ sources offer new opportunities for modelling recreation use, they also drift further away from what managers actually need to know to make decisions about recreation management, further from approaches that managers can replicate and maintain, and further from the theoretical underpinnings of recreation ecology. To that point, I came across a recent article providing a "National Model" of recreation use that I think is worth discussing in this context.

In this article ([Merrill et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26926-w)), the authors present an approach using an ensemble model of various mobile device and social media data for a variety of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Forest Service, and National Park Service sites across the country. While the scope of this analysis is impressive, I present critiques of contemporary approaches and propose a pathway for advancing recreation ecology.

**Managerial Relevance**/
First, I think it is important to acknowledge the authors' contribution. They have developed a robustly validated model that can be used to estimate recreation use in protected areas across the country. While this is a valuable tool in the absence of any data on recreation, this article falls short in its practical applications and realistic expectations of what this model can provide managers. First, many of the sites (e.g., National Park units) already generate monthly estimates of recreation use that have been mandated by law and developed by agency scientists for many decades. However, many managers of these sites would likely agree that monthly visitation estimates provide little managerially relevant information and at the unit level provide little in the way of actionable information for recreation management. This is one of my main critiques of this paper, an exercise in model development that provides little information to managers and doesn't address the need for more timely and spatially explicit information for recreation management that can inform more pressing challenges in protected area management.

**Data Abstraction**/
Second, while I authored a manuscript several years ago using mobile device data, the field should exercise caution regarding its inclusion in visitor use estimation models. My chief concern with these data arises from the fact that the providers/companies seem to rise and dissolve with little warning, each company sources its data from often unknown service providers, and the data itself is a sample that may be generalizable to the population in some areas (i.e., urban areas)—with some estimates suggesting it represents 4% of the population—but the sampling frame remains unknown, and likely not representative of recreationists in protected areas. These datasets, along with tweets and publicly shared photos, comprise this vogue trend of using "novel data" to estimate recreation use. While I don't disagree that these data sources can be useful for understanding recreation use at broad scales, I think we need to be more critical about their application in recreation management particularly given the lack of transparency, and replicability of using these data. On a more fundamental level, I think we need to be more critical about the increasing level of abstraction with all of this data. While no data source is free from bias, I am increasingly skeptical of ensemble models that attempt to correct for the biases of one data source with another data source when little or none of the data is _actually_ measuring _real_, _observed_ recreation use.

**Theoretical Underpinnings**/
Third, I think we need to be more critical about the purpose of estimating recreation use and its theoretical underpinnings in recreation ecology. In a blog post that accompanied a magazine article ([Undark](https://noahcreany.github.io/posts/2025/04/undarkarticle)), I shared one of the foundational concepts in recreation ecology, the use-impact relationship. In brief, the use we're most interested in measuring to inform how we manage recreation use is changing spatial and temporal patterns of recreation use. While agencies are required to provide some estimates of recreation use at the monthly and yearly level, these estimates are not used to inform management decisions, but instead the primary utility of these data are for Congress to justify funding for recreation management. What managers and scientists across disciplines are most interested in is fine-scale spatial and temporal patterns of recreation use. With this in mind, while a national model is ambitious, what I believe we should be working towards is finer-scale models that can help inform managers and scientists across disciplines of wildlife and land-use ecology understand spatial and temporal patterns of recreation use.

A notable exception of the use of mobile device data targeting this fine-scale and human-wildlife interaction is a recent paper from ([Ditmer et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2026.111718)) that provides some important insights for understanding the effect of human presence and noise on ungulate behavior and habituation. Nevertheless, there are many challenges that still remain for operationalizing mobile-device data or human mobility data (as it is referred to in wildlife ecology) as a covariate in these interaction models and authors call for even finer-scale data (for both wildlife and human-presence) to better understand these interactions.

**A path forward**/

My critiques of this paper and these _novel data_ are the result of my own experiences with these data and my own critiques of the field working with managers to develop applied science to inform recreation and protected area management. Nevertheless, I believe there are exciting opportunities to move forward in this field, and one such example is the use of _Strava Metro_ to provide a more fine-scale and spatially explicit estimate of recreation use. Another approach I've been working on recently is using computer-vision to count and understand recreation use. The progress in this field has been impressive even in the short-term of four or five years, and I think there are going to be some really interesting opportunities in the next few years to apply these models to understanding recreation use and providing the important information to classify activity type, group-size, speed, and other important variables to understand human behavior and presence and the interaction effects on wildlife. Fundamentally, I believe the greatest strength of the use of computer vision approaches is that they are measuring _real_, _observed_ use and can be operationalized to provide estimates of human presence or dynamic human footprint at an ecologically relevant scale.
